MeshVisor Patch

Hole repair and mesh cleanup for 3D scans, built for Blender.

What It Is

MeshVisor Patch is a Blender addon built around one specific problem: 3D scans and photogrammetry reconstructions almost always come with holes, non-manifold junctions, and degenerate geometry — places the camera never saw, spots where reconstruction failed locally, or leftover debris from processing. Fixing this by hand is slow, repetitive, and takes real topology experience.

The addon automates the whole chain: detecting a hole's boundary, filling it with triangles that match the surrounding mesh's density and curvature, transferring real surface detail onto the new geometry, and general mesh cleanup before and after.

In short: select a hole's edge, click Mark Hole Boundary, click Patch. Density, curvature, materials, and the seam are all handled automatically, with fine control available anywhere you want to step in.

Philosophy

Grounded in the literature

Every significant algorithm in this addon comes from published work on geometry processing: isotropic remeshing (Botsch & Kobbelt), curvature-adaptive remeshing (extensions such as Lv et al., 2022), quadric error metrics for simplification (Garland & Heckbert, 1997), and biharmonic smoothing for seams (an iterative approximation of the fairing approach used in Liepa, 2003). Early versions of some of these tools used simpler, self-designed heuristics — those were replaced once testing showed they didn't hold up.

Safety over sophistication

Any step that modifies geometry has a safe fallback if something doesn't converge: an unstable collapse position falls back to the edge midpoint, a degenerate curvature calculation gets simply skipped. Every operator can be undone with a single Ctrl+Z.

Progressive disclosure

The interface shows only what's relevant to the current step. Advanced tools — a manual geometry source, the sharp-edge angle threshold, the debug log — stay available but tucked away until you go looking for them, so each tab doesn't turn into a wall of options.

Verified before it reaches you

Changes to the addon go through a verification pass (compilation, import checks, static analysis for undefined names) before being delivered. That doesn't replace real testing inside Blender, but it catches a meaningful share of structural mistakes early.

Who It's For

Good fit

  • Photogrammetry scans — objects, statues, artifacts, terrain/archaeological scans with holes from camera blind spots.
  • Digital restoration and heritage conservation — where both filling the gap correctly and being able to flag what was reconstructed matter.
  • General cleanup of raw scan meshes — non-manifold geometry, duplicates, degenerate slivers left over from processing.
  • Organic meshes of small to medium size — holes up to a few thousand or a few tens of thousands of faces process at a reasonable speed.

Less suited for

  • Precision CAD modeling — for exact geometric shapes (cylinders, planes, parametric relationships), Blender's native Boolean/Solidify tools or dedicated CAD software remain the better choice.
  • Very large holes (upward of 50,000-70,000 selected faces) — the current architecture (pure Python, element by element) has a real speed ceiling; splitting a large hole into smaller sections works better.
  • Reconstruction straight from a raw point cloud — the addon operates on an already-triangulated mesh.

Installation

  1. If you have an earlier version installed: Edit → Preferences → Add-ons, find "MeshVisor Patch", click Remove — this fully clears the old folder from disk.
  2. Install the new .zip: drag-and-drop it directly into the Blender window, or use Install from Disk in the same menu.
  3. Enable the addon (checkbox next to its name).
  4. The panel appears in the 3D viewport sidebar (N), under the MeshVisor tab.
Installing a new zip over an old one without Removing it first can leave stale files behind, since the addon is now a folder containing several files. If tools seem missing or behavior looks outdated after an update, check this step first.

The three main tabs (Prepare, Patch, Remesh) map to workflow stages. Settings and Finish live in small popover windows, opened from icons in the header, so they don't compete for space in the main tab row.

  1. Prepare — check the mesh's health, repair junctions and non-manifold geometry, clean up loose parts.
  2. Patch — mark a hole's boundary and fill it.
  3. Remesh — adjust triangle density on any current selection, whether or not it's a fresh patch.
  4. Finish — final checks: normals, remaining loose geometry, one last weld pass.

Prepare Tab

This is where the mesh gets ready before marking a hole, or where general cleanup happens any time.

Mesh health

Analyze

Scans the mesh once, only when you click it, so it won't slow the UI down while the panel just sits open and shows a plain-language summary: whether any junctions are blocking a hole from being marked, plus counts of holes, loose verts/edges, and zero-area faces.

Auto Repair (One-Click)

Runs, in sequence: Weld Entire Mesh → Aggressive Auto-Repair → Analyze. A single Ctrl+Z undoes the whole pass. Handy as a quick first attempt; the individual tools below stay available if anything remains.

The Largest Hole / Smallest Hole dropdown next to Select Boundary Junctions controls what happens when boundary tracing hits a point where two holes touch tangentially: by default it picks the larger hole automatically and leaves the smaller one alone (to patch separately later if you want); flip it to catch the smaller one explicitly instead.

Patch Tab

Step 1 — Select the hole's boundary

Select Full Boundary Loop

Select 1-3 open edges around the hole, click — traces the whole outline automatically.

Mark Hole Boundary

Locks in that outline as the hole to fill (stored on the mesh, persists through saving).

Weld Distance controls how close two vertices need to be to count as the same point during boundary detection — raw scans often have separate per-face vertices that should really be one.

Step 2 — Neighborhood analysis distance

How far from the edge the addon looks to learn density, curvature, and real surface shape. 0 m means only the first row of adjacent faces. A larger radius gives Shrinkwrap more context, but doesn't by itself change the patch's target density — that comes from the density mode chosen in step 3.

Step 3 — Fill

Pick a density mode and a Surface Fit, then click Patch.

Patch runs as a background process with a progress bar (Refine → Relax → Randomize) and won't freeze the interface. Press Esc partway through to cancel.

Remesh Tab

Adjusts triangle density on any current selection, whether it's a fresh patch or existing mesh — useful for evening out or adapting an existing part of the mesh.

Advanced: Geometry Source hidden by default

By default, Remesh reads density and curvature from whatever mesh directly borders the selection. To point it at a different area instead — one with more representative detail, for example — Pick Geometry Source gives you an eyedropper tool in the viewport, with an adjustable radius (scroll to resize live).

Desired result

The same Density Mode options, plus:

  • Min Triangle Angle — a safety floor for diagonal flips (18° by default); lower gives more freedom to fix high-valence points but risks thin slivers.
  • Seam and Material — see Seam & Material.

Run

The default face limit is 20,000 (adjustable in Settings, with a warning past 50,000 since processing time grows quickly beyond that).

Presets

The dropdown at the top, visible from any tab. Each preset sets Weld Distance, Min Triangle Angle, Density Mode, Seam, Material, Surface Fit, and Aggressive Weld Distance in one click — every value can still be adjusted individually afterward.

Density Modes

Two established approaches from remeshing literature.

Curvature-Adaptive default

Finer triangles where the real neighboring surface is more curved or detailed, coarser where it's flat. The value is sampled point by point along the hole's real boundary (curvature computed from the actual dihedral angle at each point) and diffused inward. Usually much faster than Uniform, since it produces far fewer triangles overall.

Uniform (Match Neighborhood) slower

One consistent triangle size across the whole patch, matching the seam — even in flat areas that don't need that much density. Worth using when perfect regularity matters (mechanical parts, simple geometric shapes), at the cost of producing far more triangles, which makes the relax and randomize phases take proportionally longer.

If Patch seems to be taking a long time, check which mode is selected first. It isn't necessarily a bug — Uniform is inherently more expensive on large holes by design.

Surface Fit

How the patch gets projected onto the real surface to pick up its curvature and detail.

Project Along Normal default

Follows each point's normal until it finds the real surface. The search distance is bounded (relative to the target triangle size), so it can't jump an unreasonable distance. Usually faster than Nearest Surface and gives better results on complex terrain.

Nearest Surface

Searches the closest point across the entire target, with no distance cutoff — this can be considerably slower on a large neighborhood. Worth trying if Project gives a worse result on particularly tricky geometry, like very deep crevices or folds.

Seam & Material

Seam

  • Crisp — the seam stays exactly as generated, a precise, visible join.
  • Smooth — a band of a few rings on both sides of the seam gets faired through an iterative bi-Laplacian pass (this minimizes curvature variation across the band, a standard substitute for true fairing when no sparse linear solver is available), so the join reads as a smooth transition instead of a hard line.

Material

  • Blend In — the patch takes on the majority material from its surroundings and blends into the rest of the mesh.
  • New Patch Material — the patch gets its own material ("Patch Material"), visually distinct, for workflows where being able to identify exactly what was reconstructed matters.

Workflow Tips

  1. Run Analyze before anything else, so junctions get flagged before Mark Hole Boundary fails on them.
  2. For large holes (past roughly 30,000-40,000 selected faces), box-select smaller sections and run Remesh on each one separately — the current Python, element-by-element architecture has a real speed ceiling at that scale.
  3. Start with Curvature-Adaptive almost every time — it's faster and usually more faithful to the real geometry than Uniform. Save Uniform for cases where perfect regularity matters more than curvature fidelity, like mechanical parts.
  4. Project Along Normal is a solid default, but on very complex geometry (deep crevices) with visible folding, try Nearest Surface on that specific area.
  5. Run Weld Entire Mesh once, early, when working on a raw unwelded scan — this avoids running into fresh junctions repeatedly as you move between different holes on the same object.
  6. Treat a preset as a starting point — adjust individual parameters once the first result is visible.
  7. Turn on Debug Mode before a testing session where several setting combinations are being compared — the log automatically records every setting used per run, along with processing time.

Common Scenarios

Archaeological or terrain scan, very raw

Preset Fast Scan Cleanup → Weld Entire Mesh first (likely unwelded across the whole outline) → Analyze → repair remaining junctions individually if Aggressive Auto-Repair doesn't catch everything.

Small, highly detailed object (statue, artifact)

Preset Heritage Restoration → a larger neighborhood radius in Patch step 2 for richer curvature context → Crisp seam, so the repair stays identifiable.

CAD mesh, mechanical parts

Preset CAD Cleanup → density mode stays Uniform on purpose → a higher Min Triangle Angle to avoid irregular shapes → accept the longer processing time as the price of regularity.

Organic object, complex shape (terrain with folds, creatures)

Preset Organic Mesh → Smooth seam → if folding shows up on very concave areas, switch Surface Fit to Nearest Surface just there.

Very large hole (tens of thousands of faces)

Box-select smaller portions of the selection → run Remesh on each section separately → only raise the limit in Settings if you're prepared to wait (the warning appears past 50,000).

Troubleshooting

SymptomLikely causeWhat to try
Mark Hole Boundary marks the wrong, small piece The click landed on a separate fragment that happens to sit spatially close to the real hole Check the Largest/Smallest Hole dropdown; run Analyze and Repair Boundary Junctions first
Patch result has long, thin, spiky triangles Refinement stopped early (face limit reached) before finishing subdivision Raise the limit in Settings, or split the hole into smaller sections
The patched surface looks folded or overlaps itself Nearest Surface found the closest point on a different part of the surface, in a complex concave area Switch to Project Along Normal
Remesh is very slow Uniform density mode on a large selection Try Curvature-Adaptive
Progress bar stuck visually after a crash or reload The "running" state was saved into the file The "Stuck? Click here" button in the panel resets the state without touching the mesh

Debug Mode

Turned on from Settings. From then on, every Patch/Remesh run records a log line in memory: exact timestamp, processing time, every setting used (density mode, Surface Fit, distances, angles, preset), the result, and internal counters (how many edge flips were accepted or rejected, how many QEM solver fallbacks happened and why).

The Copy Log button copies the whole log straight to the clipboard — useful for comparing setting combinations quickly, or for reporting an issue with full context.

Glossary

TermMeaning
Boundary loopThe outline (loop of open edges) that borders a hole in the mesh.
JunctionA point where 3 or more open edges meet — a sign of non-manifold geometry, and a blocker for normal boundary tracing.
Non-manifoldGeometry that doesn't describe a topologically clean surface (edges shared by more than 2 faces, junctions, and so on).
ShrinkwrapBlender's native modifier, used here to project the patch onto the real surrounding surface.
QEM (Quadric Error Metrics)A method (Garland & Heckbert, 1997) for choosing the optimal merge position when simplifying an edge, derived from the surrounding face planes — keeps the real shape closer than a plain average would.
Discrete curvatureApproximated here from the dihedral angle — the angle between the normals of two adjacent faces; near 0° for flat areas, larger for curved or sharp ones.
Fairing / biharmonic smoothingSmoothing that minimizes curvature variation for a genuinely smooth transition, rather than a plain positional average.
Zero-area faceA topologically valid triangle whose three vertices are overlapping or collinear — it occupies no real surface area and can interfere with normals, UVs, or export.
Weld / merge by distanceMerging vertices closer than a given distance — corrects scans where every face carries its own unwelded vertices.
